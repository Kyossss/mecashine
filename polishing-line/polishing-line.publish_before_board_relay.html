Polishing Line

Both stations in one view, with your real shoe in three sizes. The shoe is measured on a fast first pass with the brushes parked, backed up, then polished on a second pass that never stops. The side brushes are chenille yarn: they turn and fling out when their motors are on, and droop when they stop.

Three-quarter view

What the display shows

  • One big word for the state: CALIBRATING, READY, MEASURING, REVERSING, POLISHING or DONE, each in its own colour so it can be read from across the room, with the elapsed time top right and a progress bar underneath.
  • The stored shape: while measuring, the shoe's height and width are drawn as the sensors read them, and the measured length appears when the heel has passed. While polishing, two markers show where the side brushes and the top roller are along the shoe. This doubles as a check that the measuring pass worked.
  • Three status boxes: each station's current step, its sensor reading and whether its motor is spinning, plus the belt's direction and speed. A fault would replace the big word with the reason, in red.
  • Start and Stop: indicators for the two physical buttons. The screen has no touch, so they only show the buttons' state. Start lights up green only when the machine is ready for a shoe.
  • During calibration: the shape panel becomes a checklist of the seven steps, ticked off as they finish.

The layout uses large text and flat blocks of colour so an ILI9488 over SPI can redraw only the parts that change. Redrawing the whole 480 × 320 screen every loop would slow the control loop down.

How it performs with three shoe sizes

CheckSmallMediumLarge (your model)
  • The mechanics cope with all three. Both stations reach every size within the length of the racks, and with no stops the brushes are on the shoe for roughly 85 to 95% of each pass. The misses are at the very tip of the toe and where the shoe gets wider faster than the jaws can open.
  • The sensors are the limit, not the servos. Once the brushes are on the shoe, the sensors sit very close to it: the amber rows. That is nearer than a VL53L0X can measure dependably (below about 30 mm), and the top sensor clears the tongue by only about a centimetre.
  • Option A or B. B: store the shape and work from it. Reacting to the reading immediately (option A) would lift the top roller up to about 36 mm off the shoe on every size.
  • Size changes little. A smaller shoe needs a few millimetres more jaw and carriage travel and gives the side sensor slightly more room. The top sensor is equally close on all three.
  • The cycle time is an estimate. It assumes the belt polishes at 20 mm/s and measures and reverses at 80 mm/s, the jaws move at 10 mm/s and the carriage at 40 mm/s. Your servo speeds will change it.

Start-up calibration

A 360° servo cannot say where it is. The two distance sensors can, whenever there is no shoe in the way: the side sensor sees the reflector, so its reading is the jaw opening, and the top sensor sees the belt, so its reading is the carriage height. The calibration uses them as the ruler. Switch the view above to “Start-up calibration” and press Start to watch it.

First start-up of the prototype

  1. Clear the belt. Run it for a few seconds so nothing is between the jaws or under the carriage.
  2. Home the jaws. Open until the side sensor reads the open distance to the reflector (194 mm in this model). That is how the machine knows the jaws are open, with no limit switch.
  3. Home the carriage. Raise until the top sensor reads the home distance to the belt (246 mm).
  4. Check the stop pulses. Tell both servos to stop and watch the readings. If one drifts, nudge its stop pulse until it holds. This matters most for the carriage, which hangs on its servo.
  5. Time the jaws. Close for a fixed time and see how far the reading fell, then open and see how far it rose. Both arms move, so the reading changes 2 mm for every 1 mm of jaw travel.
  6. Time the carriage. The same, down and then up. Expect two different speeds because of gravity.
  7. Time the belt with the first shoe. The two sensors are 251 mm apart along the belt. The time the toe takes from one to the other gives the belt speed: forward fast, in reverse, and at polishing speed on one dry run with the brushes parked.
  8. Save. Store the home readings, stop pulses and speeds in the ESP32's flash.

Every start-up after that, and every shoe

  • Start-up: clear the belt, home both stations against their sensors, and re-check one timing. If it has moved by more than about 10%, run the full calibration again.
  • Between shoes: once a shoe has left a station, its sensor sees the reflector or the belt again. The station re-homes against it, so error never carries from one shoe to the next.
  • During the polishing pass: the side sensor still sees the reflector while the jaws start closing, and again for the last 71 mm after the heel has passed it. Both are used to correct the jaw position. The top sensor sees the belt while the carriage starts coming down.

What replaces the limit switch

  • No change means stop. If a servo is told to move and its sensor reading does not change within half a second, something is jammed or blocking the beam. Stop everything and show the fault on the screen.
  • Every move has a time limit. Homing that takes longer than the racks allow is stopped as a fault.
  • Averaged readings. A VL53L0X is good to a few millimetres. Take about ten readings while everything is still before trusting a home position.

With no switch, a wrong sensor reading is the only thing that ends a homing move. A printed end stop on each rack would be a cheap backstop.

What the measuring pass is for

It is not there because of the pauses. It is there because the machine has to learn each shoe's shape, and it can only learn it while the brushes are parked. Once a brush is on the shoe, its sensor is as close as 9 to 10 mm (top) and 19 to 32 mm (side) from the leather, which is closer than a VL53L0X can measure dependably.

The measuring pass is also what lets the pauses go. The pauses existed to give the slow servos time to reach the shoe. With the shape already stored, the machine knows where the toe will be and how wide and high it is, so each servo starts moving before the shoe arrives and is in place when it gets there.

  1. Measure, fast. Jaws open, carriage up, motors off. The belt carries the shoe past both sensors at full speed and the shape is stored.
  2. Back up, fast. The belt reverses the shoe to the start.
  3. Polish, no stops. The belt runs forward at polishing speed. The side jaws start closing straight away and the top carriage starts coming down when the toe is about 90 mm away, so both meet the toe as it arrives. They then follow the stored shape and let go at the end.

When the motors spin

  • Side brush motors: on when the jaws start closing at the beginning of the polishing pass, off when the heel has passed the brushes and the jaws release.
  • Top roller motor: on when the carriage starts coming down, off when the roller reaches the end of the tongue and lifts.
  • Off the rest of the time: during the measuring pass and the reverse, nothing spins. Spinning brushes would throw lint at the sensors while they are measuring.
  • Direction: the two side brushes turn opposite ways, as drawn, so their sideways push on the shoe cancels.

Both sequences

The two stations overlap on the same shoe, so each runs its own steps and the main loop services both on every pass.

Side polishers

  1. Home. Open the jaws until the sensor reads the open distance to the reflector.
  2. Measure. As the shoe passes on the first pass, store its width along its length.
  3. Wait. Jaws stay open while the belt backs the shoe up.
  4. Close ahead. As the polishing pass starts, turn the brush motors on and close toward the stored toe width. The sensor seeing the toe re-checks the shoe's position.
  5. Follow. Move the jaws in and out to follow the stored width as the shoe passes.
  6. Release. When the heel has passed the brushes, stop the motors and open to home.

Top polisher

  1. Home. Raise the carriage until the sensor reads the home distance to the belt, about 246 mm.
  2. Measure. As the shoe passes on the first pass, store its height. The reading jumping deeper marks the end of the tongue.
  3. Wait. Carriage stays up while the belt backs the shoe up and the toe travels to the station.
  4. Lower ahead. When the toe is about 90 mm away, turn the roller motor on and come down toward the stored toe height.
  5. Follow. Keep the roller on the stored height up to the end of the tongue.
  6. Lift. Stop the motor and raise to home. The roller is already higher than the collar at that point, so the belt keeps moving.
  • Distances along the belt are running time. The belt servo has no position feedback, so every distance is belt speed × running time, calibrated once. Each station's sensor seeing the toe on the polishing pass cancels the built-up error.
  • Stop button or any fault: the belt stops, all motors stop, the side station jumps to Release and the top station jumps to Lift, at the same time.
  • No limit switches: both stations home against their own sensor. Nothing changes on the wiring sheet.

Assembly order

  1. Set the three buck converters first, with nothing on their outputs. Power the supply, put a multimeter on each output and turn the trim pot: XL4016 to 6.0 V, both XL4015 to 5.0 V. They often ship set well above 5 V, which would destroy the ESP32 or the display.
  2. Power off and join every ground. Supply V−, all three converter outputs, ESP32, both motor drivers, PCA9685, PCF8575, both sensors and the display.
  3. Connect XL4015 #1 to the ESP32 only. Power on and confirm the 3V3 pin reads 3.3 V.
  4. Add the I²C boards, powered from 3.3 V. Run an I²C scanner sketch. Expect 0x20 (PCF8575), 0x29 (both sensors, until the code separates them), 0x40 and 0x70 (PCA9685).
  5. Add the display and run a library test pattern.
  6. Add servo power, then one servo at a time. Connect the 6 V rail to the PCA9685 screw terminal. Test each servo off the machine first and find its stop pulse.
  7. Add the pump relay. With the pump not yet connected, run test 15 and listen for the click. Then connect the pump to COM and NO.
  8. Add 12 V to the motor drivers last. Test each brush motor with the brush off the shaft.
  9. Mount on the machine and run the start-up calibration from the logic tab with the belt empty.

Cables that move

  • On the top carriage: the lift servo, the roller motor and the top sensor all ride up and down. Leave a loop of slack for the full travel and tie the bundle to the carriage so the solder joints do not flex.
  • On the side arms: the side sensor and both brush motors move in and out with the jaws. Same again: slack loop, strain relief at the arm.
  • Sensor wires: keep each sensor's four signal wires under about 50 cm, twisted together with their ground, and routed away from the motor leads. I²C misreads next to a running motor are the most likely fault on this machine.
  • Motor and pump leads: twist each motor's pair, and solder a 100 nF ceramic capacitor across each motor's terminals. The bench test showed the side sensor resetting as soon as the brush motors start, so these capacitors are needed, not optional. The pump gets one too, plus its diode.

Use a relay module with an optocoupler input, which switches reliably from the ESP32's 3.3 V. If yours switches on when IN is HIGH, set PUMP_ACTIVE_LOW to false in the firmware's config.h. Control board

All your modules on one mounting board, seen from above, with every wire routed. It keeps the grouping from your photo (drivers on the left, ESP32 in the middle, power on the right) and adds a wire duct down the middle and a row of plugs for everything that leaves the board.

Why it is laid out this way

  • One duct, no wires over modules. Every wire drops from its module straight into the duct between the two rows, runs along it, and comes up at its destination. A slotted cable duct or a row of cable-tie mounts along that strip does the job.
  • Power on the right, signals on the left. The three converters and the 12 V input sit together, away from the sensor plugs. That keeps motor and converter noise off the I²C wires.
  • Everything that leaves the board goes through a plug on an edge. Brush motors and servos leave from the top; sensors, buttons, display and 12 V leave from the bottom. The board can then be unplugged and lifted off the machine.
  • The drivers are turned to face the duct. Each TB6612 has its control pins toward the duct and its motor outputs toward the top edge. The PCA9685 has its power terminal toward the duct and its servo plugs toward the edge.
  • Short heavy wires. The 12 V input is next to the XL4016, which carries the most current.

What changes from your board in the photo

  • Left off: the two-channel relay. It is not part of this build, so its place on the board is free.
  • Turned: the TB6612 boards and the PCA9685 are rotated so their signal pins face the duct.
  • Added: five screw-terminal plugs along the bottom (two sensors, buttons, display, 12 V in) and three small ones for the brush motors at the top.
  • Kept: the two drivers and the PCA9685 together on your perfboard, the ESP32 breakout in the middle, the PCF8575 bottom left, the XL4015 pair top right.
